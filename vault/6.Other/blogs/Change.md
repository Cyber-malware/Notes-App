In the first part, which contains the first 3 chapters, James Clear talks about how habits develop and how they become part of identity.

Habits are a double-edged sword. Bad habits can cut you down just as easily as good habits can build you up, which is why understanding the details is important. Slight changes in your life can create a big impact, like if you change yourself daily **1%**, then by the end of the year you will be **37%** better than the previous year.

Actions that are repeated over time gradually, and the brain gets rewards from them, become habits, with a curious life of their own. The more deeply a thought or action is tied to your identity, the more difficult it is to change.

If you’re having trouble changing your habits, the problem isn’t you. The problem is your system. Focusing on the overall system, rather than a single goal, is one of the core themes of this book.

![[habbit_4.jpg]]

There are three layers of behavior change. The first layer changes the outcome, like losing weight. The second layer is changing the process, like following a routine. The third layer is changing the identity, meaning beliefs, your worldview, your self-image, and your judgments about yourself and others.

Your identity emerges out of your habits, and you are not born with present beliefs. Every action you take is a vote for the type of person you want to become. Nothing happens overnight, and no single instance will transform your beliefs.

Putting this all together, you can see that habits are the path to changing your identity. Every habit you develop not only gives you results but also teaches you to trust yourself that you can do anything.

- Each time you write a page, you are a writer.
- Each time you encourage your employees, you are a leader.

![[habbit_5.jpg]]

True behavior change is identity change. You might start a habit because of motivation, but the only reason you’ll stick with one is that it becomes part of your identity. If you don’t shift the belief behind the behavior, then it is hard to stick with long-term changes. Improvements are only temporary until they become part of who you are.

- The goal is not to read a book; the goal is to become a reader.
- The goal is not to run a marathon; the goal is to become a runner.
- The goal is not to learn an instrument; the goal is to become a musician.

In the second part, **Make It Obvious**, the author starts this by explaining an experiment.  
Edward Thorndike studied learning by placing cats in “puzzle boxes” that could be escaped by performing a simple action, such as pressing a lever. At first, the cats behaved randomly, pawing, sniffing, and exploring until they accidentally triggered the mechanism and escaped to reach food.

Over repeated trials, Thorndike observed that the cats escaped faster each time. They gradually learned to associate pressing the lever with the reward of freedom and food. After 20 to 30 trials, the behavior became automatic, with escape times dropping from over a minute to just a few seconds.

From these experiments, Thorndike concluded that behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes are more likely to be repeated, while those with unpleasant outcomes fade away. This principle explains how habits form and why repeated actions become quicker and more automatic over time.

The process of building the habit can be divided into four steps: Cue, Craving, Response, and Reward.  
![[habbit_2.jpg]]

The cue is any signal that triggers the mind to behave. Our brain all the time scans the environment and looks for triggers (cues), and cues are meaningless until they are interpreted.  
The thoughts, feelings, and emotions of the observer are what transform a cue into a craving. Craving is the desire for an urge or change, and they are the motivational force behind every habit.  
While response is the action you take, and reward is the benefit you get. Like for a smoker, the smell of a cigarette will be a trigger for him, but for others it will be very bad.

The third step, which is response, is the habit you perform and takes the form of action. A habit will only occur if you are capable of doing it. Like some people don’t smoke because access to cigarettes is not easy for them, and then the last one is reward delivered in the form of satisfaction. For cue, craving, and response, it is all about reward.

You can change a habit by modifying it at any level. Like, eliminate the cue and your habit will never start; reduce craving and you won’t experience enough motivation; make behavior (response) difficult and you won’t be able to do it; and if the reward you get at the end fails to satisfy your desire, then you will have no reason to do it again. I think Islam teaches us the same thing: if you are stuck in sin, you continue to hate it, and its reward fails to satisfy your brain’s desire, so you end up giving up the sin.

![[Habbit_3.jpg]]

This fourth-step process continues all the time, with our brain predicting what happens next, trying out different responses, and learning from the results. All this happens in a few seconds.

Next, James Clear divides these four steps into two phases. The problem phase includes cue and craving, when you realize something needs to be changed, and the solution phase includes response and reward, when you take action and achieve the change you desire.  
![[habbit_6.jpg]]

Imagine walking into a dark room and flipping on the light switch. You have performed this simple habit so many times that it occurs without thinking. You proceed through all four stages in a fraction of a second. The urge to act strikes you without thinking.

James Clear talks about researchers who studied three groups to understand exercise habits. The first group was the control group and simply tracked their exercise. The second group tracked workouts and learned about the health benefits of exercise.

The third group received the same motivation but also made a specific plan for when and where they would exercise. Specifically, each member of the third group completed the following sentence: 
**During the next week, I will partake in at least 20 minutes of vigorous exercise on [DAY] at [TIME] in [PLACE]**.

Only 35–38% of people in the first two groups exercised weekly, while 91% of those who made a clear plan exercised at least once per week, showing that planning was far more effective than motivation alone.

**When situation X arises, I will perform response Y.**

That is implementation intention. You can also write down the exact time. Like for me, I decided to exercise at least 10 minutes daily but didn’t define the time, so in the morning I said I will do it later, and at night I forgot and slept without doing it. So I can bind myself like every morning, for 10 minutes, I will do exercise.

We tell ourselves, “I’m going to eat healthier” or “I’m going to write more,” but we never say when and where these habits are going to happen. We do not define our goals clearly; that’s why we can’t develop any habit.

**I will [BEHAVIOR] at [TIME] in [LOCATION]**

Same as I defined earlier, that I will exercise for 10 minutes every morning. Another best way to build a habit is to identify the habit you already do and then stack your new behavior on top. This is called habit stacking.

**After [CURRENT HABIT], I will [NEW HABIT].**

After the Fajar Namaz, I will exercise for 10 minutes daily.

![[habbit_8.jpg]]

This will continue after one habit to another habit. Strategies like implementation intention and habit stacking are the best ways to design a plan for when and where to take action.
