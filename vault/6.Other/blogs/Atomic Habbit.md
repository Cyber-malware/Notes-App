Edward Thorndike studied learning by placing cats in “puzzle boxes” that could be escaped by performing a simple action, such as pressing a lever. At first, the cats behaved randomly pawing, sniffing, and exploring until they accidentally triggered the mechanism and escaped to reach food.

Over repeated trials, Thorndike observed that the cats escaped faster each time. They gradually learned to associate pressing the lever with the reward of freedom and food. After 20 to 30 trials, the behavior became automatic, with escape times dropping from over a minute to just a few seconds.

From these experiments, Thorndike concluded that behaviors followed by satisfying outcomes are more likely to be repeated, while those with unpleasant outcomes fade away. This principle explains how habits form and why repeated actions become quicker and more automatic over time.
